      Foundations · Foundation · Original practice material

      Enterprise value, equity value and cash flow

      Enterprise value measures the operating business under a specified valuation convention. In a simplified cash-free, debt-free deal, equity value equals enterprise value less debt plus cash. Actual price adjustments depend on the agreed definitions of cash, debt, debt-like items and working capital. EBITDA is not cash flow: capital expenditure, tax and working capital may consume cash.

      Applied explanation and practical exercise

      In a transaction, first separate operating-business valuation from the cash paid to shareholders. For example, an enterprise value of £200m and net debt of £45m imply £155m equity value before other adjustments. A target with £10m EBITDA may still need £6m capital expenditure and £3m additional working capital; EBITDA alone is a poor proxy for available debt service. A locked-box price uses a historical balance sheet and leakage protections; completion accounts adjust against agreed closing metrics. Neither mechanism guarantees a particular price without the definitions.

      Practical exercise

      Prepare a one-page bridge from enterprise value to equity consideration, identifying net debt, debt-like items, working capital and leakage. Explain how a disputed debt-like item affects buyer and seller differently.

      A business has £120m enterprise value, £35m debt and £5m cash. Ignoring other adjustments, what equity price is implied?

      Model answer and reasoning

      £120m − £35m + £5m = £90m. The SPA must specify which items count as cash and debt.

      Foundations · Intermediate · Original practice material

      Debt, interest rates and covenants

      Floating-rate borrowing commonly references a benchmark plus margin. Rate rises can reduce interest cover, increase refinancing pressure and reduce the debt a buyer can service. Fixed-rate debt and hedging may change the impact. A covenant breach depends on the precise financial definitions, test dates and cure rights, not on a headline rate alone.

      Applied explanation and practical exercise

      Suppose £80m of floating debt pays a benchmark plus 300 basis points. A benchmark increase affects interest expense, while the margin may depend on leverage or rating. In a leveraged acquisition, a higher rate can reduce cash available for amortisation or distributions and affect covenant headroom. Review the definition of EBITDA, permitted add-backs, interest coverage, testing periods and cure provisions. A borrower can hedge some benchmark risk, but hedge break costs and mismatched tenors can create new exposure.

      Practical exercise

      Draft a lender diligence checklist: facility amount, tenor, rate, financial covenants, mandatory prepayment, security, hedging and change-of-control terms.

      A borrower has £80m of unhedged floating-rate debt. Assuming immediate full pass-through, what is the additional annual interest from a 25bp rise?

      Model answer and reasoning

      25 basis points = 0.0025; £80m × 0.0025 = £200,000 before other changes.

      How clients make money

      Revenue is sales before expenses; profit depends on costs and accounting rules. Operating cash flow also reflects collection and payment timing. A high-growth company can be profitable on paper but short of cash. Ask what customers pay for, what drives costs, how working capital behaves and what regulation constrains the business.

      Applied explanation and practical exercise

      Trace a company's cash conversion cycle from purchasing inventory to collecting sales. If a business invoices £100m and collection slows by 30 days, the additional receivables balance could be material even if the profit margin is unchanged. Distinguish fixed costs from variable costs, recurring from transactional revenue, and customer concentration from overall sales growth. In diligence, compare contractual payment terms, termination rights and actual collections. A facility may fund a seasonal working-capital gap, but it cannot fix structurally unprofitable operations.

      Practical exercise

      Choose a company and explain its customers, revenue drivers, largest costs, working-capital cycle and two contractual or regulatory risks.

      Sales rise but customers take much longer to pay. What can happen?

      Model answer and reasoning

      Receivables tie up cash. A profitable business may still need working-capital finance.

      Private equity and management rollover

      Sponsors seek returns through operating improvements, growth, capital structure and eventual exit. Rollover means existing investors or management reinvest some proceeds or retain an interest.       Infrastructure and project finance

      Project finance often relies on cash flows of a project company, with limited or non-recourse structures depending on the agreed financing. Construction, operating, revenue and political risks are allocated across contracts, insurance, security and sponsor support. A final investment decision is not the same as financial close or operational completion.

      Applied explanation and practical exercise

      A project company's debt service depends on construction completion and operating cash flows. A lender may examine EPC obligations, completion tests, delay damages, revenue arrangements, operating contracts, insurance, reserves and direct agreements. A final investment decision signals sponsor approval; financing documentation, drawdown conditions and construction risk may remain.       Securitisation and credit risk

      A securitisation uses a financing structure in which investors are paid from a pool of underlying exposures, subject to contractual priorities. Analyse asset transfer, security, servicing, cash-flow waterfalls, credit enhancement and insolvency risk. CMBS can expose investors to property cash flow and refinancing risk.

      Applied explanation and practical exercise

      Consider senior and junior notes backed by a pool of loans. The waterfall allocates available collections according to priorities; junior exposure may absorb losses first, subject to the actual structure. Asset eligibility, transfer or security mechanics, servicing, liquidity support, account control and insolvency remoteness matter. A rating addresses defined credit risks under assumptions, not every legal or market risk. In CMBS, property income, tenant concentration and refinancing of the underlying loans can affect note performance.

      Practical exercise

      Draw a simplified payment waterfall and explain how a missed borrower payment reaches different investors. Identify two legal assumptions that require verification.

      Legal due diligence and SPA risk allocation

      Diligence maps material legal risks to commercial responses. A known issue may justify a price adjustment, specific indemnity, closing condition, covenant or decision not to proceed. Warranties allocate contractual risk subject to the negotiated disclosure and liability framework; they are not a substitute for verification.

      Applied explanation and practical exercise

      First establish whether the agreement really contains a change-of-control trigger and whether the proposed structure activates it. Identify notice requirements, consent discretion, termination rights and the revenue or operational dependence on that counterparty. A consent condition may be appropriate for a critical contract; for less material contracts, a covenant, price adjustment or targeted protection may suffice. Disclosure can qualify warranties but does not itself obtain consent. Record the issue, evidence, materiality, recommendation and unresolved questions.

      Commercial contracts: warranties, indemnities and caps

      A warranty is a contractual statement; an indemnity can allocate specified losses under its wording. Liability caps, baskets, exclusions, time limits and mitigation provisions require careful interpretation. A contract's exact terms matter more than labels.

      Applied explanation and practical exercise

Other responses include escrow, retention, price adjustment, remediation or a closing condition. Consider disclosure, insurance, enforceability, counterparty credit and double recovery. Avoid promising that a label automatically yields full compensation.

      Law firm economics and client value

      A commercial firm sells expertise, judgment and execution capacity. Fee arrangements may include hourly rates, fixed fees or other structures. Profitability depends on utilisation, staffing, realisation and overhead; a high-value matter is not necessarily a high-margin matter. Clients value predictable scope, risk control and timely outcomes.

      Applied explanation and practical exercise

      A client chooses counsel for expertise, execution, risk judgment and cost predictability. On a fixed-fee transaction, additional jurisdictions, unexpected diligence or repeated negotiation may change the assumptions underlying scope. A lawyer should identify the change, explain commercial and legal implications, propose options and obtain appropriate agreement before significant out-of-scope work. Efficient delegation, knowledge management and accurate time capture support both service quality and profitability. A large transaction value does not determine the legal fee or margin.
